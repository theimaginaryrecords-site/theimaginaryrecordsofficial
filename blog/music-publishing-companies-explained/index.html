Music Publishing Companies Explained

Every song has two halves: the recording (the master) and the composition (the song itself — melody and lyrics). Music publishing companies manage the composition half. They make sure songwriters get paid whenever their songs are used, and they work to create new opportunities for those songs. Here is how they work.

What publishers do

Publishers manage a songwriter's rights in a track. They license the music to others, collect payments from third parties, register copyrights, index tracks with performing rights organisations, and handle accounting and distribution. Publishers also pitch songs to performers and producers — a job historically called song plugging — hoping a performer records the song and makes it a hit.

The big three publishers

The three largest publishing companies in the world are affiliated with the three largest record companies: Sony Music Publishing (part of Sony), Universal Music Publishing Group (part of Universal Music Group), and Warner Chappell Music (part of Warner Music Group). Their size comes from the success of the recording artists affiliated with their parent companies, plus vast catalogues of classic songs.

Types of publishing companies

Publishing companies fall into four main categories. Major publishers are the big three above. Major-affiliated publishers are independents with deals with the majors to handle licensing administration. Independent publishers handle their own administration in-house and are self-funded. Writer-publishers are songwriters who handle their own publishing, sometimes hiring someone to handle administration as an employee.

How publishers make money

Publishers earn money through licensing fees and royalties. In a typical deal, the publisher gets a share of the copyright — often around 50% — in exchange for exploiting the song commercially. Publishers also handle administration: collecting revenue on the songwriter's behalf, registering works, and making sure royalties are counted correctly. A publisher's job is to protect the composer's interest, because that is how the publisher makes its money.

Do you need a publisher?

If you write your own songs, publishing matters even if you never sign a publishing deal — someone has to register your songs and collect your royalties. Many independent songwriters start as their own publishers, then sign with a publisher or administrator once the catalogue grows. The key rule: always get legal advice before signing any publishing agreement.

Administration deals: a lighter option. Not every songwriter needs a full publishing deal. An administration deal is lighter: the administrator handles registration, licensing, and collection for a small commission, without taking ownership of your copyrights. For independent songwriters who want to keep control, administration is often the smarter first step before considering a co-publishing or full publishing agreement.
