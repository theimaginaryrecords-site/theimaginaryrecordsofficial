Music Royalty Splits: How to Split Earnings Between Collaborators

Most songs today are written by more than one person. Producer, topline writer, lyricist, featured artist — each one has a claim to a share of the royalties. How you split that money is one of the most important business decisions in a song's life, and it must be made before the song earns anything, not after. There is no industry-standard formula: splits are whatever the collaborators agree to. What matters is that the agreement exists, is written down, and totals 100%.

Equal splits vs. proportional splits

The most common approach, especially in pop, hip-hop, and EDM, is equal division: two writers split 50/50, three split 33.3% each, four split 25% each. This "everyone in the room gets an equal slice" approach — sometimes called the Nashville rule — prevents arguments and is the safest default for new collaborators. Proportional splits divide by actual contribution: for example, a primary songwriter who wrote lyrics and melody takes 60%, the producer who made the beat takes 30%, and a featured vocalist who co-wrote the bridge takes 10%. A common framework is 50% to the lyricist/melody writer and 50% to the music maker. Typical ranges reported by industry guides: a beatmaker contributing an instrumental only, roughly 20–33%; a full producer who co-wrote the song, roughly 40–50%; a lyricist only, roughly 20–33%.

The split sheet

A split sheet is the written record of who owns what percentage of a song. It should list every contributor's legal name, role, contact details, PRO affiliation, publisher (if any), percentage share, and signature, along with the song title. Sign it in the session or immediately after — before the song is released, pitched, or played for anyone. If collaborators later disagree and there is no split sheet, PROs, labels, and publishers may hold the royalties until the dispute is settled, which means nobody gets paid.

Two splits, not one

Remember that a song has two separate splits: the publishing split (who owns the composition) and the master split (who owns the recording). They are governed by different agreements. A featured vocalist who only performed — and did not write — typically gets no songwriting share, but may negotiate master points or a flat fee. Producers commonly receive 2–4% master points in traditional deals, separate from any songwriting share. Do not accidentally give away recording rights when you meant to agree on songwriting, or vice versa.

Practical rules

Agree on splits the day the song is written. Put it in writing and get signatures. Use your distributor's split-payment features (many distributors now pay collaborators automatically) so nobody has to chase anyone. And be generous but honest: the music world is small, and a fair split today is worth more than a disputed hit tomorrow.
