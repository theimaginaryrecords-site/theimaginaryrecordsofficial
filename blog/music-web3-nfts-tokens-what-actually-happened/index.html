Music and Web3: NFTs, Tokens, and What Actually Happened

By Aabid Khan · October 14, 2026

Music and Web3: NFTs, Tokens, and What Actually Happened

A few years ago, music NFTs were supposed to revolutionise the industry. Artists would sell digital collectibles directly to fans, cut out middlemen, and earn royalties on every resale. Then the hype collapsed. Here is what actually happened — without the hype, and without the doom.

The big moment

In March 2021, Kings of Leon became one of the first major acts to release an album as a collection of NFTs. Their album When You See Yourself went on sale as tokens containing exclusive artwork and a limited-edition vinyl version, plus golden ticket auctions guaranteeing front-row seats to headline shows. The band generated over $2 million from the sale, including more than $500,000 donated to Live Nation's Crew Nation fund supporting live music crews during the pandemic. Around the same time, DJ and producer 3LAU generated more than $11 million in under 24 hours from an NFT drop tied to his album Ultraviolet.

The promise

The pitch was real. NFTs could let artists sell music, videos, artwork, tickets, and merchandise directly to fans. Smart contracts could pay the artist a royalty on every secondary sale — something streaming never offered. For smaller artists earning a few thousand dollars per million streams, the idea of selling a limited digital collectible for real money was genuinely appealing.

Why it stalled

Reality was harder. Most music fans were newcomers to crypto and NFTs, and Kings of Leon had to extend their sale by 24 hours because fans struggled to buy. Prices were out of reach for most fans, and most marketplaces only accepted payment in Ethereum. As one industry voice put it at the time, the NFT business was a collectibles business going through a hype moment like the dot-com boom — a rush, then a shakeout. Gas fees, poor user experience, and negative sentiment led to stagnation, and without a strong secondary market, music NFT sales declined.

What survived

The collapse cleared out speculation, but useful ideas survived. Digital collectibles, fan tokens, and blockchain ticketing are still being explored — with simpler payment, clearer ownership, and real utility like concert access. The lesson is not that Web3 was fake; it is that technology only matters when fans can use it easily and artists benefit clearly.

The honest takeaway

Music NFTs were neither a revolution nor a scam. They were an early experiment that proved fans will pay for scarcity and access — and proved that confusing technology kills demand. Artists considering Web3 today should ask one question: does this make life better for my fans? If the answer is no, it is not worth doing.
