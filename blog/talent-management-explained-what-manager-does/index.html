Talent Management Explained: What a Manager Does for an Artist

Every successful artist has someone behind the scenes making sure the career moves in the right direction. That person is usually the manager. But what does a music manager actually do — and when does an artist need one? Here is the full picture.

The manager's job

A talent manager, also called an artist manager or personal manager, guides the professional career of an artist. They oversee day-to-day business affairs, advise on professional matters, make long-term plans, and coordinate the team around the artist. They choose which opportunities to take, negotiate deals, hire lawyers and agents, and act as the artist's main point of contact for business decisions.

Types of managers

There are three main types. The personal manager handles overall career strategy and coordinates everything. The business manager handles the financial side — taxes, investments, budgeting, and accounting — and usually charges around 5% of gross income or a flat monthly retainer. The road or tour manager handles logistics during tours: day-of-show operations, travel, crew coordination, and on-the-ground problem solving, usually for a weekly salary or per-show fee.

How managers get paid

Personal managers typically work on commission, usually between 15% and 20% of the artist's gross income, though some sources cite a range of 10% to 20%. Because their income is tied to the artist's success, their interests are aligned — they earn more when the artist earns more. That alignment is the core of the relationship.

Manager vs. agent

Managers and agents are different roles. A manager advises on the whole career and coordinates the team. A booking agent's job is narrower: securing live work. In some places, like California, managers legally cannot procure employment for an artist — that is the agent's licensed job. The manager plans; the agent books.

When do you need one?

Early on, many artists self-manage while building traction — releasing songs, pitching playlists, growing an audience. A manager becomes valuable once opportunities multiply: bookings, label talks, collaborations, brand deals. A good manager believes in the music before the numbers, and helps the artist focus on creating while the business runs properly.

Red flags to watch for. Not every manager is a good one. Be cautious of managers who demand large upfront fees instead of commission, promise guaranteed fame, or want to control your creative decisions without discussion. A real manager earns from your success, communicates clearly, and puts agreements in writing. If something feels wrong, trust your instincts and get a lawyer to review the deal.
