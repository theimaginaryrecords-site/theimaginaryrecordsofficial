Playlist Culture: How Playlists Changed What We Listen To

There was a time when you discovered music through radio, friends and record shops. Today, a playlist decides what you hear next — and that shift has changed not just listening, but the music itself. Playlist culture is the defining force of the streaming era, and every artist needs to understand how it works.

From radio to algorithm

Spotify, founded in 2006, built its empire on playlists. Early on, the company discovered that many listeners used streaming as background accompaniment — for workouts, commutes, even sleep — and were happy to outsource the choice of what to hear next. It hired editors to build playlists and nudged passive listeners toward them. Today, streaming accounts for 84% of global recorded-music revenues, and playlists sit at the centre of how songs reach ears.

Editorial, algorithmic, and "algotorial"

Modern playlists blend human and machine curation. Spotify's editorial team selects pools of songs, and algorithms personalise the order and mix for each listener — a hybrid researchers call "algotorial" curation. Personalised lists like Discover Weekly, Daily Mix and Release Radar mix familiar tracks with new recommendations based on listening history. Features like AI DJ and Daylist push automation further, while Discovery Mode lets artists accept lower royalty rates in exchange for algorithmic promotion.

How it changed the music

Playlists changed incentives for musicians. The 30-second threshold to trigger a royalty payment encouraged "streambait" — songs with early hooks designed to stop listeners skipping. Critics identified a "Spotify-core" sound: pensive, mid-tempo songs that fit many playlists at once. For artists, the lesson is practical: pitch your unreleased tracks through Spotify for Artists, build your own playlists, and remember that the algorithm rewards songs people actually finish.

Gatekeepers, old and new

Playlists did not eliminate gatekeepers — they replaced them. Spotify's RapCaviar once launched careers overnight, and a single placement could transform an unknown artist. Around 2020, the balance shifted: Spotify's CEO Daniel Ek acknowledged that users increasingly preferred algorithmic suggestions over editorial picks, and the company doubled down on features like AI DJ and Daylist. Spotify's global head of editorial, Sulinna Ong, has continued to reaffirm the company's commitment to curated playlists — but the evidence shows data playing a larger role in curation every year. The gatekeeper is now part human, part machine.
