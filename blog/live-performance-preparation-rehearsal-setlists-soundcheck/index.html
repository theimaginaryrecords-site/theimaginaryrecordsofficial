Live Performance Preparation: Rehearsal, Setlists, and Soundcheck

A great live show is decided long before the lights go down. Preparation — rehearsal, setlist design, and soundcheck — is what separates a confident performance from a stressful one. Here is how professionals prepare.

Rehearse the show, not just the songs

Run the full set multiple times, including transitions between songs and any talking points. Practice performing, not just playing — the way you move, breathe, and speak between songs matters as much as the notes. In the week before the show, memorise the setlist and know every part cold, so adrenaline cannot derail you.

Build a setlist with flow

A good setlist is a story: open strong to grab attention, build energy with peaks and valleys, and place your strongest song near the end. Mix tempos, keys, and emotional content — avoid putting similar songs back to back. Keep separate setlists for different show lengths, and send the setlist to your bandmates ahead of time so everyone prepares.

Soundcheck is for sound, not rehearsal

A soundcheck tests and tunes the audio — it is not a full rehearsal. Play at the same volume you will use during the show so the engineer can set accurate levels. Use a song that covers all your instruments and vocals, and communicate in plain language: "more vocals in my monitor" beats "it sounds weird." Be professional with the sound engineer — they control how the audience hears you.

Pack for everything

Bring backups: spare cables, picks, sticks, strings, and batteries. Tape your setlist to the stage floor. Bring water, a tuner, and your merch. Arrive early, know your changeover time, and warm up vocally and physically before you go on.

The day-of checklist

Confirm load-in details a few days beforehand. On the day: arrive on time, soundcheck, set up merch, eat, warm up, and then perform. Preparation removes the variables you can control — so the only thing left on stage is the music.

Every live show has a plan B. Keep a backup of your setlist on your phone, carry spare cables and batteries, and know what you will do if a track fails or an instrument breaks mid-song. Artists who have a fallback stay calm when things go wrong — and the audience usually never notices the problem at all.
