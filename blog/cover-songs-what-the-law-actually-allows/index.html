Cover Songs: What the Law Actually Allows

Cover songs are one of the best ways for a new artist to get discovered. But uploading a cover without the right licence can get it removed — or worse. Here is what the law actually allows, and what you need before you release a cover. This is general information, not legal advice.

The mechanical licence

When you record and distribute your own version of someone else's song, you generally need a mechanical licence. In the United States, Section 115 of the Copyright Act provides a compulsory mechanical licence: once a song has been publicly released, anyone can record and distribute their own version, provided they notify the copyright owner and pay the statutory royalty rate. You do not need the songwriter's permission for a straightforward cover — but you do need to pay. The arrangement must not change the song's basic melody or fundamental character.

Streaming, downloads, and video are different

The rules vary by format. For streaming, services handle mechanical royalties through systems like the Mechanical Licensing Collective (MLC) in the US, and some distributors offer cover-song licensing that handles the paperwork for you. For downloads and physical copies, licensing must be secured before distribution. Video is a different matter entirely: posting a cover on YouTube pairs music with visuals, which requires a sync licence — and there is no compulsory sync licence. Many covers stay up because publishers choose to monetise them through Content ID, but monetisation and legal clearance are not the same thing.

Covers, remixes, and samples are not the same

A cover is your own performance of a song without changing its lyrics or core melody. A remix or anything involving sampling is a different category and usually requires direct permission from the rights holders. Changing the lyrics, translating a song, or substantially reworking it can take it outside the scope of a standard mechanical licence. When in doubt, get proper clearance before you release.

The safe way to release a cover

Use a licensing service or a distributor that offers cover-song licensing, and secure the licence before your release date — not after. Keep proof of licensing, and check whether your licence covers international distribution if you plan to release worldwide. Done properly, covers are a legitimate, low-risk way to grow your audience.
