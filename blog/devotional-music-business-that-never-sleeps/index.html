Devotional Music: A Business That Never Sleeps

By Aabid Khan · October 14, 2026

Devotional Music: A Business That Never Sleeps

Devotional music is often treated as a side corner of the Indian music industry. The numbers say otherwise. From temple loudspeakers to stadium kirtan concerts, from bhajan apps to wedding playlists, devotional music reaches every age group, every region and every income level in India. It is one of the few genres that never goes out of season — and in 2026, it is becoming one of the most dynamic live formats in the country.

Bhajan clubbing goes mainstream

The biggest story is bhajan clubbing: devotional songs performed with concert production, DJ-style energy and dance floors, but without alcohol. According to Forbes India, ticket prices for bhajan concerts average Rs 700 to Rs 2,500, with VIP and fan-pit tiers at Rs 4,000 to Rs 6,000. A white paper by MSEED estimates conservative annual revenues of Rs 50–100 crore for the format by 2030, with optimistic projections reaching Rs 200–500 crore if it keeps expanding across Tier 2 cities and diaspora markets.

Why the economics work

Devotional concerts have structural advantages. Production costs run lower than conventional festivals because of alcohol-free operations and simpler staging, while sponsorships, merchandise and food and beverage add revenue on top of tickets. EY's report "A Studio Called India 2025" projects that concerts in India will rise from around 14,470 in 2024 to over 24,500 by 2030, with devotional concerts emerging as a distinct niche within that growth.

The digital devotion economy

The business extends far beyond live shows. Between January and September 2026, religious and spiritual apps in India recorded 14.6 crore downloads, with Sanatan Status Bhajan Mantra leading by monthly active users. For artists, devotional music offers something rare: an audience that is loyal, multi-generational and always listening.

Devotion at scale

The live format is scaling fast. Media reports say at least five major devotional concerts in Delhi in March 2026 drew tens of thousands of attendees, with one event at Purana Qila drawing 7,000 people, and Delhi's government launched bhajan clubbing events on university campuses. Artists like Radhika Das have toured nine-city kirtan runs filling arenas in Indian metros and international destinations including Dubai and London, while the format is also spreading into weddings, with bhajan clubbing built into haldi ceremonies and sangeets. Devotional music is no longer a side stage — it is a headline act.
