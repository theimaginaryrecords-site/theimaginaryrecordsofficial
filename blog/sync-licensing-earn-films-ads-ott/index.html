Sync Licensing: How to Earn From Films, Ads, and OTT

Sync licensing is the business of placing your music in visual media — films, TV shows, adverts, video games, trailers, and OTT series. For independent artists, it is one of the most lucrative income streams in music: a single placement can pay more than a year of streaming. And unlike streaming, sync pays an upfront fee plus backend performance royalties. It is also one of the most misunderstood: there is no fixed rate card, and the deals that look simple on paper have real legal machinery underneath.

Two licences, one placement

Nearly every sync placement requires two separate licences. The sync licence clears the composition (the song itself — lyrics and melody), and the master licence clears the actual recording. If you wrote and recorded the song yourself and own both sides, you control both licences — a position called "one-stop" — and supervisors love it, because clearance is fast. If a label owns your master or a publisher owns part of your composition, all parties must agree before the placement can close.

What sync placements pay (estimates)

No trade body publishes a verified sync rate card, so all figures are practitioner estimates. Industry practitioner Ari Herstand's widely cited ranges for independent artists run roughly $500 to $20,000 for television, $10,000 to $80,000 for films and trailers, $2,000 to $10,000 for video games, and $20,000 into the high six figures for commercial campaigns. A $10,000 placement for an emerging artist on a Netflix show is reported as not unusual. At the low end, independent films have offered as little as a few hundred dollars. Your fee depends on usage, prominence, term, territory, exclusivity, and how badly they want your specific song.

The backend: cue sheets and performance royalties

The upfront fee is only part of the money. When your music airs on TV or a streaming series, it also generates performance royalties — but only if a cue sheet is filed correctly with your PRO. Cue sheets are the documents productions use to report which music aired, for how long, and how it was used. Missing or inaccurate cue sheets delay or reduce royalties, so confirm with the production or supervisor that one was filed.

How to become sync-ready

Have clean rights (a signed split sheet, identified publisher, a master owner who answers email), instrumental versions of every track, high-quality WAV files, accurate metadata, and realistic expectations. Music supervisors work on tight deadlines — a great song with messy rights loses to a good song that can be cleared in an afternoon. Pitch through sync libraries, licensing platforms, and supervisors directly, and keep your catalog organised so you can say yes fast.
