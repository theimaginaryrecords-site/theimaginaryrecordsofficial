Sampling in Music: Legal Rules and Famous Cases

Sampling built entire genres. Hip-hop, electronic music, and much of modern pop run on borrowed drum breaks, vocal chops, and flipped melodies. But sampling someone else's recording without permission is one of the fastest ways to get a song taken down — or to end up in court. Here is how the rules actually work. This is general information, not legal advice.

Two clearances for every sample

Like sync licensing, sampling requires clearing two rights: the master recording and the underlying composition. The label (or whoever owns the recording) controls the master, and the publisher (or songwriter) controls the composition. You generally need permission from both. If you replay a melody yourself instead of lifting the recording, you remove the master side of the deal — but you still need to clear the composition. That re-recorded approach is called an interpolation, and publishers still take a share.

The cases that set the rules

In 1991, Biz Markie was sued for sampling Gilbert O'Sullivan's "Alone Again (Naturally)" without permission, and the court ruled against him — a landmark moment that ended the era of casual, uncleared sampling in mainstream music. In Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films (2005), a US appeals court ruled that even a two-second sample of a sound recording required a licence, famously instructing artists to "get a licence or do not sample." On the other side, VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone (2016) found that a tiny, unrecognisable horn sample in Madonna's "Vogue" did not require a licence, and in 2019 the European Court of Justice ruled that permission was required for recognisable samples, while modified, unrecognisable samples could be used without authorisation.

What this means for producers today

The practical rule is simple: if a listener can recognise the source, clear it. Courts in different regions have reached different conclusions about tiny or unrecognisable snippets, which means relying on grey areas is a gamble. Clearance costs depend on how recognisable the sample is, how much of your track relies on it, and how big the release is expected to be. For independent artists, the safest options are royalty-free sample packs, original recordings, or interpolation with a proper publishing split agreed in advance.

Protect your own work too

Clearance is a two-way street. If you are the artist being sampled, the same rules protect you — nobody can legally lift your recording or melody without your permission. Registering your works, keeping dated session files, and having clear collaborator agreements make it far easier to enforce your rights if someone samples you without clearing it. Understanding sampling law protects your music whether you are the sampler or the sampled.
