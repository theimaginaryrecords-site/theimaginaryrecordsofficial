Music Copyright Basics Every Indian Artist Must Know

Copyright is the foundation of every rupee a musician earns. It decides who gets paid when your song is streamed, performed, or used in a film. Yet many Indian artists release music without understanding what rights they actually own. Here are the basics every artist should know. This is general information, not legal advice.

One song, several copyrights

A song is not a single work under Indian law — it is a bundle of rights. The lyricist owns the literary work (the lyrics), the composer owns the musical work (the melody), the singer holds performer rights in their performance, and the producer holds rights in the sound recording. Each contributor can hold copyright in their own part. That is why clear agreements between collaborators matter: without them, nobody is sure who can license what.

The Copyright Act, 1957

India's primary copyright legislation is the Copyright Act, 1957. It grants creators exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, perform, and communicate their work to the public. Protection is automatic from the moment an original work is created — registration with the Copyright Office is not required, though it is recommended because it provides stronger legal proof if a dispute ever arises. Copyright in a musical work in India lasts for the lifetime of the author plus 60 years, while copyright in a sound recording lasts 70 years from the year of first publication.

Collective management and royalties

Because millions of songs are played every day, individual artists cannot track every use of their work. That is where copyright societies come in. These organisations manage and license copyrighted works on behalf of authors, track usage, and collect royalties for their members. Joining the right society and registering your works is one of the most practical steps an artist can take to make sure performances and broadcasts actually turn into payments.

Protect yourself before you release

Keep dated records of your work — demos, lyric sheets, session files, and messages with collaborators. Put splits in writing before release, not after a song becomes successful. And never sign away your rights without understanding what you are giving up: an assignment transfers ownership, while a licence only grants permission for specific uses. Knowing the difference can protect your entire catalogue.
